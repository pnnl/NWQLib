## Expectation and sampling

Hoeffding, W. (1963), *Probability Inequalities for Sums of Bounded Random Variables*, Journal of the American Statistical Association 58(301), 13–30. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1963.10500830), [Paper](https://www.cs.rpi.edu/academics/courses/spring06/random/hoefding.pdf). The linked scan reproduces the journal pages, and page numbers below are journal pages. Theorem 1, Eq. (2.3), p. 15, gives P(mean - mu >= t) <= exp(-2 n t^2) for independent 0 <= X_i <= 1 and 0 < t < 1 - mu. The paragraph after Eq. (2.4) on that page replaces t by t/(b-a) when a <= X_i <= b, so outcomes in [-1,1] give exp(-n t^2/2). Theorem 2, Eq. (2.6), p. 16, gives both bounds directly for every t > 0, with b_i - a_i = 1 or 2. Eq. (1.4), p. 13, adds the lower tail, which doubles the bound for a two-sided deviation. The Expectation shot selection and binary intervals cite Theorem 1, and the Lanczos Gram sampling bound cites Theorem 2. The count screen that admits a stall-split valley in QHD box refinement applies Theorem 1 with both tails and a union bound over cells and levels. The exponential bound behind it is Eqs. (4.11) and (4.16), p. 22, in the proof of Theorem 2, which the paper notes also gives a direct derivation of Eq. (2.3). The finite-shot interval and joint-mass bound of QHD box refinement ([Proposition 49](mathematics.md#r49)) applies Theorem 1 and Eqs. (1.4), (2.3) with a union bound over the contiguous index intervals of every axis and over levels. NWQLib's binary inference uses the bounded-variable convention X in [-1,1]. Weighted Pauli sums apply the triangle inequality and a union bound over the predeclared setting family. Sampling-model premises and binary64 evaluation remain explicit, as described in the [expectation guide](algorithms/expectation.md).

## ADAPT-VQE

Gradient measurement references distinguish generator-spectrum assumptions, commuting measurement groups, and shot costs. Grimsley et al., arXiv:1812.11173v2, Section II.B, define the gradient-screening loop. Anastasiou et al., arXiv:2306.03227v3, Eq. (2), write each gradient as the commutator expectation ⟨[H, A]⟩, and their Sections III.A–III.E discuss commuting groups and conditional cost comparisons. Wierichs et al., arXiv:2107.12390v3, Sections 3.2–3.5 and Appendix B, give finite-frequency derivative rules. A primitive two-shift rule does not automatically apply to an entire spin-adapted unitary. Schuld et al., arXiv:1811.11184v1, Sec. III.A, Theorem 1, Eq. (8) and Eqs. (13)–(14), give the two-eigenvalue identity behind the Pauli-insertion derivative, and their Sec. III.B, Eqs. (16)–(17), give the decomposition of a derivative over unitaries, here the Pauli terms of a generator. NWQLib inserts each Pauli factor after the complete generator exponential. The comparison pools follow Romero et al., arXiv:1701.02691v2, Eqs. (8)–(9) and (17) (UCCSD singles and doubles), Yordanov et al., arXiv:2011.10540v2, Eqs. (17)–(18) (qubit excitations), and Ramôa et al., arXiv:2407.08696v3, Sec. II.B.3, Eqs. (23)–(24) (OVP-CEOs).

## Linear Combination of Hamiltonian Simulation (LCHS)

An, Childs, and Lin, arXiv:2312.03916v2, give the kernel in Eq. (7), the composite quadrature in Eq. (61) and the pointwise decay in Eqs. (185)–(186). (62) bound with Lambert W. ACL's Lemma 11 step rule, Eqs. (64)–(65), assumes T·max‖L‖ ≥ 32/e, so NWQLib selects Gauss panels with Trefethen's ATAP Theorem 19.3. An, Liu and Lin, doi:10.1103/PhysRevLett.131.150603, give the original LCHS identity with the Cauchy kernel, which ACL restate as Eq. (5) and NWQLib offers as `cauchy_density`. (6) with the parameters of Theorems 2–4. The constant-source remainder combines DLMF Eqs. 3.5.19 and 3.5.21, and product-formula steps use Childs et al. (2021), doi:10.1103/PhysRevX.11.011020, Propositions 9–10, listed under Subroutines. The algorithm guide states the scope of NWQLib's finite quadrature and error information.

## Lanczos

Kirby et al., arXiv:2208.00567v4, Sections 2.1–3.1, Eqs. (2)–(4), (6)–(7) and (13)–(27), give the signed Pauli encoding, the qubitized walk, the Chebyshev pencil and the parity readout. NWQLib uses a coherent SELECT-basis readout variant. Oumarou et al., arXiv:2603.15552v1, Section 2, Eqs. (1) and (4), give the shift and rescaling. Their Eqs. (14)–(15) assemble the projected matrices from moments, and Eqs. (16)–(18) read the moments from walk powers. Section 3.3.2, Eqs. (59)–(60), motivates sensitivity allocation. Epperly, Lin, and Nakatsukasa (arXiv 2110.07492v2, listed under GCIM), Algorithm 1.1, define overlap thresholding. NWQLib's pilot-dependent allocation and independent main samples have the conditional variance argument described in the [Lanczos guide](algorithms/lanczos.md). Its default cutoff uses empirical Gram noise for exploratory regularization. It separately reports a bound from Hoeffding's Theorem 2 (doi:10.1080/01621459.1963.10500830) and a union bound on the sampled moments entering the Gram matrix, and its explicit confidence policy cuts at twice that bound. The real-THC construction in Oumarou Appendix A.3 is distinct from the signed-Pauli encoding used here.

## Quantum Hamiltonian Descent (QHD)

Leng et al., arXiv:2303.01471v1, Eq. (1), defines the time-dependent Hamiltonian, and their Eq. (C.4) is NWQLib's cubic schedule. Kushnir et al., QHDOPT, arXiv:2409.03121v1, give NWQLib's default quadratic schedule as an example in Sec. 2.1 and report that schedules of this form work well for many test problems. The shifted cubic schedule is Eq. (15) of Wu et al., arXiv:2605.12066v1, Sec. VI, p. 7. Liu et al., arXiv:2607.16996v1, Eq. (92), write the cubic schedule with s = 1 as their QHD-C schedule, and their code runs the shifted cubic form under that name. Eq. (E.3) and Algorithm 1 describe first-order product-formula evolution, and Eqs. (F.7) and (F.9) give the finite-difference stencil and grid potential. Leng et al.'s Eq. (C.3), p. 32, is the first-order pseudo-spectral step with the left-endpoint weights of their Eq. (C.2), p. 31, and NWQLib's split-step flavor applies its symmetric (Strang) form (doi:10.1137/0705041), with which Wu et al., Sec. VI, p. 7, simulate their benchmarks. Leng et al.'s Algorithm 1, p. 44, meshes the box with the endpoint-exclusive grid of their Eq. (E.1), p. 43, which NWQLib's periodic grid uses, and its step 3 names the uniform and a Gaussian state as possible starts. Their Eq. (F.15), p. 47, gives the diagonal potential of d variables. Eq. (F.36) defines the tensor-product Hamming encoding, which NWQLib does not use. Wu et al., arXiv:2605.12066v1, Section IV.A, Eqs. (9)–(10), give the one-hot occupation-operator encoding that NWQLib's potential follows. Liu et al., arXiv:2607.16996v1, Sec. III, Eq. (12), give the one-hot hopping Hamiltonian with the wrap term that NWQLib's periodic grid uses, and their Eq. (94) gives the position mean and standard deviation in the final state, which QHD analysis reports conditional on a valid outcome. Their Sec. IV A, Eqs. (53)–(54), expand a support-local potential block in Pauli-Z strings, which NWQLib's binary encoding offers as Walsh-rotation synthesis beside the exact phase diagonal of Shende, Bullock and Markov, arXiv:quant-ph/0406176v5, Theorem 7. Their Sec. IV E, Eqs. (89)–(91), give the quadratic low-momentum kinetic phase with an unsigned momentum index, and NWQLib uses the signed index. Coppersmith, arXiv:quant-ph/0201067v1, gives the approximate quantum Fourier transform that the binary kinetic factor can select. The augmented-Lagrangian layer takes its equality terms and multiplier update from Wu et al., Sec. III.B, Eqs. (6)–(8), and its inequality term from the Powell–Hestenes–Rockafellar form of Rockafellar, doi:10.1007/BF01580138. Wu et al. keep the penalty fixed or increase it by update rules that they cite from Nocedal and Wright, doi:10.1007/978-0-387-40065-5, and the layer's `every_iteration` option reproduces the growth after every round of their code. The layer's tentative multipliers, default penalty test and multiplier safeguard follow Algorithm 4.1 and Eqs. (4.7)–(4.9) of Birgin and Martínez, doi:10.1137/1.9781611973365, its scaled multipliers their Eq. (10.4), its effective objective their Eq. (10.3), Sec. 10.1, p. 114, and its stopping test and stationarity diagnostic their Sec. 10.2.2, Eqs. (10.6)–(10.8). The docstrings of the layer also cite the book's Problem 4.6 (p. 38) for placing a bound constraint in the box or among the relaxed constraints, Problem 4.8 (p. 38) for the multiplier update from a squared slack, and Problem 4.9 (p. 39) for the alternative V of the complementarity test. For the conditions of the book's convergence results, which the layer states and does not claim, they cite Assumption 5.1 (p. 41), Theorems 5.1 and 5.2 (pp. 41–42), Assumption 6.1 (p. 48), Sec. 7.5 with Assumptions 7.6 and 7.7 (p. 64), and Sec. 7.7 with Theorem 7.2 and Assumption 7.9 (p. 70). Box refinement normalizes each marginal by the valid mass and keeps on each axis an interval that holds a chosen fraction of it, as in Wu et al., Sec. V, Eqs. (12)–(13), p. 7. Their Eq. (13) states no tie rule, so the tie rules are NWQLib's, and their Eq. (14), p. 7, lets each kept point represent the cell to its right, where NWQLib uses the cell centered on it. Neither Wu et al. nor Liu et al. link their code. Liu et al. make additional information available from the corresponding author on request, and the experiment code behind the published results of Wu et al. has no public location. The QHD guide and docstrings name files of either code only to state which settings it ran. Li, Fan and Han, arXiv:2508.02969v1, also combine an augmented Lagrangian with QHD. They simulate the QHD dynamics on classical hardware with the simulated bifurcation algorithm. The T estimate of a QHD circuit uses the leading term `3 log2(1/eps)` of the typical T count of one z-rotation from Ross and Selinger, arXiv:1403.2975v3. Al-Mohy and Higham's algorithm (doi:10.1137/100788860) computes the matrix-exponential action in the classical kernel, and its error analysis supports the kernel's mass window.

## Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE)

Ding and Lin, arXiv:2211.11973v2, Eq. (2), define the quantum complex exponential least-squares (QCELS) objective, and Eqs. (11)–(12) eliminate its amplitude. NWQLib implements one level. Theorems 1–2 assume squared overlap p0 > 0.71, and Section IV adds Fourier filtering for smaller overlaps. For statistical phase estimation (SPE), Wan, Berta, and Campbell, arXiv:2110.12071v2 PDF Eqs. (A1)–(A2), HTML Eqs. (16)–(17), give the Fourier filter of their approximate CDF, Eq. (6), and their Algorithm 1 compares the sampled approximate CDF with the threshold η/2. Their Theorem 3 with PDF Eqs. (A6)–(A7) and (A12), HTML Eqs. (21)–(22) and (27), gives the filter domain that NWQLib checks. The SPE controlled-evolution backend has a separate synthesis model from the paper's randomized LCU compiler. Kshirsagar, Katabarwa, and Johnson, arXiv:2209.11322v3, Algorithm 1 and Eq. (7), supply the largest sampled Fourier coefficient for randomized Fourier estimation (RFE), and their Theorem 2.1 gives a sample count that the finite default does not meet. Granade and Wiebe, arXiv:2208.04526v1, Algorithm 1 and Eq. (7), supply the Gaussian moments and one-bit updates for random-walk phase estimation (RWPE). The [QPE guide](algorithms/qpe.md#implementation-map) gives the exact versions, code owners and the RWPE feedback-sign derivation, and distinguishes basic RWPE from Algorithm 2's unwinding. Cleve, Ekert, Macchiavello, and Mosca, arXiv:quant-ph/9708016v1 Section 5, Fig. 6 and Eq. (5.1), give the phase register that the coherent QPE subroutine prepares with controlled powers of U. For an eigenvector input, their Eqs. (5.2)–(5.4) show that the inverse QFT then yields the nearest phase estimate on the register's grid with probability at least 4/π². The [coherent QPE API](api/subroutines/qpe.md#source-map) maps their bit order to Qiskit's.
